The quarterly review is going fine until the client's marketing director asks the question: "So what are we doing with AI?" Your account lead says the team is exploring some options. Everyone nods. Three weeks later the client mentions, casually, that another firm pitched them an AI assistant for their sales team.

That question is not a request for a demo. It's a test of whether you are still the people they call first. Agencies that answer it with a concrete offer are in a better position to keep the account. Agencies that answer it with "we're looking into it" may keep it too, but they have just told the client to look elsewhere for the next budget line.

Answering well doesn't require an engineering department. It takes a clear first offer, a scope you can deliver and a partner arrangement that protects the relationship you spent years building.

## Build, refer or partner

There are four realistic ways to respond. Each one decides who the client sees, what you need on your side and where the risk sits.

| Option | Who the client sees | What you need | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build in-house | You | Engineers, a delivery process, someone on call after launch | Payroll before demand; a team that's idle between projects |
| Refer out | Someone else | A trusted contact | The client relationship moves with the work |
| White-label | You, only | A project lead and a partner behind you | Partner quality and partner behavior |
| Co-delivery | You and a named partner | A project lead who wants to learn | Shared credit; clear roles needed |

The part agencies underestimate about building in-house is what happens after launch. An AI system that answers customers or drafts content needs monitoring, knowledge updates when the client's offer changes, and someone to look at the cases it got wrong. That's an operating commitment that outlives the project. If you hire for it, you hire for years.

Our view: partner on the first few projects, keep the relationship and the margin, and decide on hiring only once you can see which offers your clients actually buy. Those are the three models on our [Partners page](/services/partners/): **White-label Build** (we design, build and run the system; your client only sees you), **Co-delivery** (we join your team openly; you lead strategy, we lead the build) and **Referral Partnership** (you introduce, we deliver directly and keep you informed).

> **Rule of thumb:** If you want AI to become a service line, start with white-label or co-delivery. If you only want a good answer when a client asks, a referral is honest and simple.

## What to sell first

The best first offer sits next to something you already deliver, has a clear owner at the client and can be measured against a baseline. Four offers fit agency work most naturally. Here they are in the order we'd consider them for a typical marketing agency.

**An AI search visibility (GEO) audit.** Some buyers now ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity which company to hire, and your client wants to know whether its name comes up. An audit shows which buyer questions mention the client, which cite competitors instead, and why. There's no system to run afterwards, which makes it an easy first purchase, and it fits inside an SEO retainer. Our [GEO guide](/blog/generative-engine-optimization-guide/) explains what gets measured.

**An AI content engine.** If you already write for the client, this extends the retainer: a brand-voice model trained on their best material, an editorial calendar, drafts every week and a human approval step. The risk is quality, which is why the approval step is not optional. See how we approach it in [AI content without the slop](/blog/ai-content-without-the-slop/).

**A lead-response agent.** If you run the client's paid media, you already own their lead problem: inquiries that wait hours for a reply. An agent answers in minutes, asks the qualifying questions and books the call. It's higher value and higher stakes, because it talks to the client's customers. Start it with human approval on anything that isn't a routine answer.

**An internal assistant for the client's team.** Web and digital studios can deliver this with a site launch: an assistant trained on the client's documents and policies that answers their staff's questions. Lower risk, because the users are employees, and a natural monthly service.

| First offer | Fits | First version | What can go wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| GEO audit | SEO, content, PR agencies | Fixed question set, baseline, prioritized fixes | Over-claiming what fixes will do |
| Content engine | Content and social agencies | One channel, weekly drafts, approval flow | Generic output if the voice model is thin |
| Lead-response agent | Performance agencies | One inquiry channel, approval on edge cases | Wrong answers to real customers |
| Internal assistant | Web studios, IT firms | One team, one document set | Outdated documents, stale answers |

We'd pick one. Two first offers means two sets of sales materials, two delivery playbooks and half the learning.

## Scope a process, not "AI"

Most AI proposals fail in the scope, not the build. "An AI assistant for customer service" promises everything and defines nothing. A deliverable scope names one process, its inputs, its outputs, who approves what, and what is explicitly out.

Here is the one-page structure we use when we help partners write a proposal:

```text
Job:          Reply to new web and email inquiries within 10 minutes,
              answer standard questions, qualify, and offer a call slot.
Inputs:       Website form, shared inbox, service and pricing sheet
              (client-provided), calendar.
Outputs:      Reply sent or drafted, lead record updated in CRM,
              call booked or handed to sales with a summary.
Approval:     Anything about discounts, complaints or custom quotes
              goes to a named person before sending.
Out of scope: Phone calls, existing-customer support, other languages.
Baseline:     Median first-response time and booked calls per 100
              inquiries, measured for 4 weeks before launch.
Review:       30 days after launch, against the baseline.
```

Notice what isn't there: a promise of "40% more bookings". You don't control the client's sales team, their offer or their market. You do control whether the system answers fast, accurately and within its rules, and whether you measured where things started. Promise the measurement, not the result.

On timing: a white-label build typically runs 3 to 10 weeks per project, depending on scope, and a single agent like this one is usually live in 3 to 5 weeks. Start the baseline measurement the week you sign, so it runs in parallel with the build instead of delaying launch.

Price it the way it behaves: a setup fee for the build and an ongoing fee for operation, because the system needs care every month. You set your own price to the client. Selling it as a one-off project is how agencies end up supporting a system for free.

## Who does what in delivery

Ambiguity about roles is what makes white-label feel risky. Write the split down before the kickoff. This is how it typically divides in a white-label build:

| Stage | Your agency | Sandbox, behind you |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery and scoping | Leads the client conversation | Joins calls as part of your team; drafts the technical scope |
| Proposal and price | Owns it, sets the price | Inputs on scope, timeline and risks |
| Access and data | Requests it from the client | Specifies exactly what's needed and how it's protected |
| Build | Reviews every milestone | Designs, builds and tests |
| Launch and client training | Presents, under your brand | Prepares materials and supports live |
| Operation | First line with the client | Monitoring, fixes, knowledge updates |
| Monthly report | Delivers it | Produces the data and the draft |

In co-delivery the table looks similar, but we are visible in the room and part of the work is teaching your team to do more of it.

Your side needs one thing above all: a project lead who understands the client's business. Not an engineer. Someone who can tell when an answer would embarrass the client, and who has the authority to say so.

## Protect the relationship, in writing

The fear that stops most agencies is simple: the partner meets your client and, a year later, sells to them directly. Handle it in the agreement, before anyone meets anyone.

- **Non-solicitation.** The partner doesn't contact, pitch or contract with your clients directly during the partnership and for an agreed period after, unless you ask.
- **One channel.** You decide who speaks to the client and when. Partner staff use your domain and your templates if you want them to.
- **Branding.** Dashboards, documents and reports carry your brand.
- **Exit plan.** If the partnership ends, what happens to running systems: handover, transition support and who owns what. The client owns its data either way.

There's one more piece, and it's where white-label gets misunderstood. Your brand on the work does not make the people processing the data invisible.

If your client is in the EU and the system handles personal data, the GDPR says a processor can't engage another processor without the controller's prior written authorisation, specific or general ([Article 28(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)). In practice: your delivery partner goes on the client's sub-processor list. That's normal and rarely an issue, as long as it's disclosed.

The EU AI Act adds a second angle. It defines a "provider" to include whoever puts an AI system into service under its own name or trademark ([Article 3](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)). Sell a customer-facing assistant under your brand in the EU and you may carry provider duties, including the transparency rule that people must be told they're talking to an AI, which has applied since 2 August 2026 (Article 50). This is not legal advice; check with counsel for your markets. But design every customer-facing agent to say it's an AI, everywhere, and the question mostly goes away.

## Pilot with one client

Don't launch an AI service line. Run one pilot and let it tell you whether you have one.

Pick a client with a good relationship, a named owner on their side, a process with real volume and data you can actually get access to. Avoid the client who asked loudest if they can't name who will review the work.

> **Example:** A hypothetical 40-person performance agency manages ads for a regional home-services company. The client gets around 300 web inquiries a month, and the agency can see in the CRM that the median first reply takes several hours. The pilot: one lead-response agent on the web form only, approval required for anything about price, a 4-week baseline, then launch and a 30-day review. The agency's own time goes into scoping, reviews and the client meetings; the build and operation sit with the partner.

At the 30-day review, look past the client's numbers and at your own. How many hours did your team spend? Was the account lead comfortable presenting it? Would the client buy a second process? If yes on all three, you have a repeatable offer and the start of a delivery playbook. If not, you learned that for the cost of one project instead of a team.
