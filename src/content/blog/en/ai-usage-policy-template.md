Picture an account manager with a client call in twenty minutes and a 30-page contract draft she hasn't read. She pastes it into the AI chat app on her phone, asks for the five riskiest clauses, and walks into the call prepared. Nobody told her not to. Her company's policy, if she could find it, says "use of generative AI tools requires prior approval from IT", and nobody has ever asked IT anything.

That contract is now sitting in a personal account on consumer terms. The company doesn't know it happened, and neither does the client.

Nobody in that scene is being careless. She is getting her job done with the only tools and rules she has. A stricter document won't change that. A shorter one might, if it answers the questions people actually have mid-task and is taught with examples from their own jobs. Below is the one-page policy we'd start from, line by line, plus a template you can copy. It's practical guidance, not legal advice.

## Why bans and twenty-page policies both fail

A ban fails because the tools are already on everyone's phone. Usage doesn't stop; it moves to personal accounts where you can't set terms, see what was shared or help anyone use it well. You trade a manageable risk for an invisible one.

The long policy fails for a different reason. It's written to protect the company in a dispute, so it's full of definitions, scope clauses and "shall not" sentences. It answers the lawyer's question ("were employees informed?") and ignores the employee's question ("can I paste this into that?").

The test we use is simple: **can a new hire apply the policy in five seconds, mid-task, without opening a PDF?** If not, the policy exists on paper only. That pushes you to one page, written in the language your team uses, with examples instead of categories.

## The account matters more than the brand

The most common mistake is treating "ChatGPT" or "Gemini" or "Copilot" as one thing. The same model can come under very different terms depending on the account you use.

Business tiers from the major providers usually come with contractual commitments on training and data handling. OpenAI, for example, [states on its business data page](https://openai.com/business-data/) that by default it does not use data from its business products or its API to train its models. A personal account signed up with a Gmail address runs on consumer terms that the user, not the company, agreed to, and settings the company can't check.

So the core rule of the policy is about accounts, not brands:

- **Approved tools, on company accounts, for work data.** List them by name, with who can get a seat.
- **Personal accounts only for public information.** Using a personal account to rewrite a public blog post is fine. Using it for a client's contract isn't.
- **No unapproved add-ons** that connect to email, drive or calendar. Browser extensions and "AI note-takers" that join meetings are where data leaves quietly.

If you only enforce one line of the policy, make it this one. It also forces a useful decision: you have to actually pay for and provision the approved tools, or the rule is a ban in disguise.

## A data traffic light people can apply in five seconds

Classification schemes with four or five levels fail in practice because nobody remembers the definitions. Three colors work because each one maps to a single action.

| Color | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Green** | Any approved tool. Personal accounts allowed. | Published marketing copy, public websites, general research questions, your own rough notes with no names |
| **Yellow** | Approved tools on company accounts only. Remove what you don't need. | Internal documents, meeting notes, project plans, anonymized customer feedback, drafts of proposals |
| **Red** | Not in general AI tools at all, unless a specific system was approved for it. | Client confidential material under NDA, personal data of customers or employees (IDs, health, payroll, bank details), passwords and keys, unannounced financials, anything a contract says can't go to third parties |

Two rules make the table work in real life. First, **when in doubt, treat it as the next color up**. Second, **write the examples from your own business**. A clinic's red list mentions patient records and appointment notes; an agency's mentions client briefs and ad account credentials. Generic examples get nodded at and forgotten.

> **Example:** A sales lead wants help with a follow-up email. Asking "how do I follow up with a prospect who went quiet after a demo?" is green. Pasting the call notes with the prospect's name and budget into the company account is yellow, which is fine. Pasting the prospect's signed NDA and pricing sheet is red.

Red means "not in a general-purpose chat tool", which is narrower than "never with AI". A system built for that data, with the right contract, permissions and logging, can be approved for it. That approval is a specific decision, made once, not an improvisation.

## What a human must review before it leaves the company

AI drafts are often good enough to send, which is exactly the risk. The policy should name the outputs that always get a human check, and who does the checking.

Our list for most companies:

- Anything going to a client, a candidate or the public.
- Any number, date, price, name, quote or citation. Models produce plausible specifics that are wrong.
- Statements with legal, medical, financial or safety weight.
- Code that runs in production or touches customer data.
- Decisions about people: hiring screens, performance notes, credit or eligibility.

The responsibility rule matters more than the list: **the person who sends it owns it**. Not the tool, not whoever wrote the prompt. "The AI got it wrong" should land the same way as "my spell checker got it wrong". In our view this sentence does more than any warning about hallucinations, because people check what they're accountable for.

## Clients, confidentiality and ownership

Three questions come up early in almost every rollout, so the policy should answer them before they're asked.

**When do we tell clients AI was involved?** We wouldn't stamp every email. Disclose when a contract requires it, when a client asks, and when AI produced a substantial part of work the client pays for as expert judgment. A research summary that a consultant checked and signed is the consultant's work. A report that is mostly model output with light edits deserves a sentence of disclosure.

**What do our contracts already say?** Many NDAs and service agreements limit sharing client information with third parties, and a cloud AI provider can count as one. Before red-list decisions, someone should read your top five client contracts. It's an afternoon of work and it often moves items from yellow to red.

**Who owns what AI produces?** Less than people assume. In the US, the Copyright Office's [January 2025 report on copyrightability](https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html) concluded that purely AI-generated material isn't protected, while human contributions like selection, arrangement and modification can be. Rules differ by country. The practical line for the policy: don't promise clients exclusive ownership of AI-generated assets without human creative work on top, and don't use AI to imitate a specific artist, competitor or copyrighted work. If this matters to your business, talk to your lawyer.

## The one-page template

Copy this, replace the brackets and cut anything that doesn't apply. If it grows past one page, something belongs in a separate document.

```text
[COMPANY] AI USAGE POLICY                         Version [1.0] · [Month Year]
Owner: [Name, role]          Questions: [channel or email]       Next review: [date]

1. PURPOSE
We want everyone to use AI to do better work, faster. This page says which
tools, which data and which checks. If something isn't covered, ask the owner.

2. APPROVED TOOLS AND ACCOUNTS
- Approved: [Tool A – company account], [Tool B – company account], [...]
- Work data goes ONLY into approved tools on company accounts.
- Personal AI accounts: public (green) information only.
- No AI add-ons, browser extensions or meeting note-takers connected to
  company email, drive, calendar or calls without approval.

3. DATA TRAFFIC LIGHT
GREEN  – Any approved tool. Examples: [published content, public research].
YELLOW – Company accounts only; remove names/details you don't need.
         Examples: [internal docs, meeting notes, proposal drafts].
RED    – Not in general AI tools. Examples: [client material under NDA,
         customer/employee personal data, passwords, unannounced financials].
When unsure, treat it as the next color up.

4. HUMAN REVIEW BEFORE IT LEAVES THE COMPANY
A person checks every AI-assisted output that goes to clients, candidates or
the public; every number, name, date, quote and source; anything with legal,
financial, medical or safety weight; production code; decisions about people.
The person who sends it owns it.

5. TELLING CLIENTS
Disclose AI use when a contract requires it, when a client asks, or when AI
produced a substantial part of a deliverable. Never claim a human did work
that AI did unchecked.

6. CONFIDENTIALITY AND OWNERSHIP
Client contracts and NDAs come first; if they limit third-party processing,
that data is red. Don't promise exclusive ownership of AI-generated assets
without human work on top. Don't use AI to copy a specific person's or
company's protected work.

7. NEW TOOLS AND NEW USES
Request via [form/channel]: what tool, what task, what data color, who uses it.
The owner answers within [5] business days. Until then, the answer is no.

8. OWNERSHIP AND REVIEW
[Owner] maintains this page and reviews it every quarter: approved tools,
red-list examples, questions received, incidents. Report mistakes (a paste
into the wrong tool, an unchecked output sent) to [owner] the same day. Early
reports are not punished; hidden ones are taken seriously.
```

The last line of section 8 is deliberate. If confessing a mistaken paste gets someone in trouble, you'll never hear about one, and you need to hear about them to fix the rules. For section 7, the [12 questions to ask before an AI system touches your company data](/blog/ai-vendor-security-questions/) are a good checklist for evaluating any tool that lands in the request queue.

## Rolling it out: where we'd start

Don't email the policy. A PDF announcement gets a few "thanks" replies and changes nothing.

We'd run it as a 45-minute session per team. Before it, collect ten real situations from that team: the contract summary, the candidate email, the spreadsheet of customer complaints. In the session, people sort each one into green, yellow or red and say what review it needs. The disagreements are the useful part. When two people put the same task in different colors, you've found a line the policy needs to make clearer.

Then put the page where the work happens: pinned in your team chat, linked from the AI tool's workspace, printed by the desk if that's your culture. Log every question people send the owner for the first month. They're the raw material for the first quarterly review.

If you use AI systems in the EU, there's a regulatory reason as well. Article 4 of the EU AI Act has applied since February 2025. The 2026 Digital Omnibus on AI softened its wording: providers and deployers must now take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff and others using AI on their behalf, rather than guarantee a "sufficient level" of it. According to the [European Commission's AI literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers), national market surveillance authorities supervise and enforce it from 2 August 2026. A policy that people were actually taught, with a record of the sessions, is a sensible part of that work. Check the specifics with counsel.

A policy is the floor, not the goal.
