The workshop went well. Forty people, two hours, a presenter who made the AI write a sonnet about quarterly targets and then summarize a 60-page report in seconds. People laughed, asked good questions and left saying they'd try it.

Six weeks later someone pulls the usage report. A handful of people use the tool every day. Most opened it twice in the first week and never again. Nobody is against AI. They just went back to their inboxes, and nothing from the session survived contact with a normal Tuesday.

This is the default outcome of AI training, and it isn't the trainer's fault. The format is built to impress, not to change how work gets done. Below is what separates training that sticks from training that entertains, and a four-week plan you can run with one team.

## Why the demo fades

Training sticks when it changes what someone does on a specific, recurring task. Most AI sessions never get that far, for reasons that are mechanical rather than motivational.

**The examples aren't the person's work.** A sonnet or a generic marketing email shows that the tool is capable. It doesn't show an accounts payable clerk what to do with the 40 vendor emails waiting for her. The translation from demo to own task is the hard part, and it's left as homework nobody does.

**Nothing is left behind.** People leave with a vague memory and maybe a slide deck. They don't leave with a saved prompt that already works on their weekly report, so the second attempt starts from zero, and the second attempt is where most people quit.

**No time, no permission, no rules.** If nobody knows whether client data can go into the tool, the cautious majority won't try. If the manager never mentions it again, the signal is that this was optional.

**No feedback.** Someone tries, gets a mediocre draft and concludes the tool is mediocre. Nobody shows them that a better instruction, an example and a short checklist would have fixed it.

There is decent evidence that format matters. In BCG's 2025 survey of more than 10,600 workers in 11 countries, people who received five or more hours of training, especially in person and with coaching, were [more likely to become regular users](https://www.bcg.com/press/26june2025-beyond-ai-adoption-full-potential). The same survey found only 36% of employees felt adequately trained. Hours alone won't fix it, but the gap between a two-hour demo and a coached program is real.

## Train each role on its own work

The single biggest change is to stop training "the company" and start training roles. A sales coordinator, a paralegal and a plant supervisor need different exercises, because the tasks that eat their week are different.

Before any session, collect three to five recurring tasks per role. A short survey and a couple of 20-minute interviews are enough. You're looking for work that is frequent, mostly text or spreadsheets, and easy to judge as good or bad.

| Role | Tasks worth training on | What "good" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Customer support | Reply drafts for the ten most common questions; ticket summaries for handoff | Correct policy, right tone, no invented promises |
| Sales | Call prep from CRM notes; follow-up emails after a meeting | Uses what the client actually said; clear next step |
| Operations | Weekly status report from a spreadsheet; supplier email triage | Numbers match the source; exceptions flagged |
| Finance / admin | Invoice query replies; variance explanations | Figures checked against the ledger before sending |

Then build every exercise on real (and permitted) material: an anonymized ticket, last month's actual report, a real call transcript. If an exercise uses an invented sample document, it's teaching the demo, not the job.

> **Rule of thumb:** If a participant can't name the day and time they'll next use what they practiced, the exercise was too generic.

## Build the assets while you train

The best test of a session is what exists afterwards that didn't exist before. We design each one so that the exercises produce assets the team keeps using.

Three kinds matter most.

**A shared prompt library.** Not a list of clever tricks: a small set of working instructions for the team's actual tasks, each with an owner. A useful entry has a fixed shape:

```
Task: Weekly ops status report
Owner: [name]
When to use: every Friday, after the production sheet is closed
Input: export of the production sheet (no customer names)
Instruction: [the prompt that works, with one example of a good report pasted in]
Check before sending: totals match the sheet; every red item has an owner and a date
Last reviewed: [date]
```

**Configured assistants.** Most business AI tools let you save an assistant with standing instructions and reference files. A support team with an assistant that already knows the returns policy and the house tone starts every reply closer to done.

**Review checklists.** Short lists of what a human must verify before AI-assisted output leaves the building. These matter more than prompts, because they're what keeps quality stable when people get comfortable.

By the end of a good training cycle, the team has 5 to 15 library entries, one or two assistants and a checklist per output type. That's the material a new hire gets on day one.

## Who keeps it going after the trainer leaves

Content doesn't sustain adoption. People do, and two roles carry most of the weight.

**A champion per team.** Not the most technical person: the one others already ask for help. Give them a defined job (maintain the library, run a 15-minute show-and-tell every two weeks, collect what isn't working) and protected time to do it, a couple of hours a week at first. Train them more deeply than everyone else. This is the train-the-trainer idea, and it's what lets training scale beyond the first team.

**A manager who uses it visibly.** If the head of operations opens the Monday meeting with "I drafted this summary with the assistant, here's what I had to fix," the team learns three things at once: it's expected, it's allowed and it needs checking. In the same BCG survey, only 25% of frontline workers said their leaders gave enough guidance on AI. Modeling is the cheapest guidance there is.

Then close the loop. A practice cycle we like:

1. Each person uses a library entry on a real task during the week.
2. They share one output (good or bad) in the team channel.
3. The champion and one peer review it against the checklist.
4. Whatever went wrong becomes an edit to the prompt, the assistant or the checklist.

Two or three of these cycles turn a session into a working method. They also surface the tasks where AI isn't helping, which is just as useful to know.

## Measure adoption like a product team

"We trained 120 people" is an activity, not a result. Neither is "we have 120 licenses." These are the measures we'd track instead.

- **Weekly active use:** the share of trained people who used the approved tool on a work task at least once a week, tracked for at least eight weeks. Check whether your tool's admin console reports active users; if it doesn't, a one-question weekly check-in in the team channel does the job.
- **Tasks moved to AI:** a named list of recurring tasks now done with AI by default, per team. This is the number that compounds.
- **Quality sample:** each week the champion reviews a handful of AI-assisted outputs against the checklist and logs pass, minor fix or reject.

> **Example:** A hypothetical 12-person support team is trained in October. By week six, 10 of 12 use the assistant weekly, four ticket types are drafted with AI by default, and the weekly sample of ten replies shows eight passing and two needing a policy correction. That last figure is the useful one: it points to the one library entry that needs fixing.

If weekly use drops after week three, don't run another workshop. Ask the champion which task people stopped using it for, and why.

## Leaders need a different session

Putting the leadership team in the staff workshop wastes their time and blurs the point. Leaders don't need to write better prompts. They need to make better decisions about where AI goes in the business.

A leadership session should cover:

- **Where AI fits and where it doesn't:** how to pick processes by volume, risk and how easy the output is to check.
- **Risk:** what data can go where, what needs human approval, what happens when output is wrong.
- **Investment:** how to size a project, what a believable return looks like and what to stop doing.
- **Leading the change:** what they will model, what they will measure and what they will stop tolerating.

There's also a compliance angle for companies operating in Europe. Since February 2025, Article 4 of the EU AI Act has obliged providers and deployers of AI systems to act on the AI literacy of their staff. The Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since July 2026, softened the wording: companies must take measures to support AI literacy, and no specific level is mandated, but the obligation stands. The Commission's [AI literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers) adds that no certificate is required, that simply asking staff to read instructions for use may be ineffective, and that supervision and enforcement rules apply from 2 August 2026. Role-based training, with an internal record of who was trained on what, is a sensible way to show you took it seriously. (This is not legal advice; check with your counsel.)

## A four-week plan to start with one team

Here's the shape we'd use for a first team. It assumes a half-day kickoff and about two hours a week of the team's time after that.

| Week | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (prep) | Survey roles, collect 3-5 real tasks per role, confirm usage rules | Task inventory, approved tools and data rules |
| 1 | Hands-on kickoff on real tasks; pick a champion | 5-8 prompt library entries, one configured assistant |
| 2 | Practice on live work; first share-and-review cycle | Revised entries, first review checklist |
| 3 | Extend to the next most frequent tasks; manager demo in team meeting | Library at 10-15 entries, checklist per output type |
| 4 | Review adoption data and quality sample; decide what's next | One-page adoption report, list of tasks moved to AI |

Week 4 often reveals something bigger: a task so repetitive and well-defined that it shouldn't be done with an assistant at all, but handed to an agent. That's a different project, and [choosing the first process for an agent](/blog/choosing-your-first-ai-agent/) has its own rules.
